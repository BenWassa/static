# 06 Capacity Model

## Why small things become too much

### Opening

At the start of the day, you can handle things.

The lights are annoying but manageable. The message that needs an answer can wait. The meeting is tiring but survivable. The change in plan is frustrating, but you adapt.

Then, later, something small happens.

A pen drops. A noise repeats. Someone asks what should be an easy question. A plan changes by ten minutes. Suddenly the reaction is larger than the event. Your body floods, freezes, snaps, or shuts down.

It looks like the small thing caused the reaction.

It did not. The small thing arrived at threshold.

The capacity model is the difference between trigger and cause. The trigger is what happened last. The cause is everything that accumulated before it.

### The simple truth

Autistic capacity is not only about how much stress a person can tolerate. It is about how much sensory, social, cognitive, and emotional processing the nervous system can perform before the system crosses threshold. Ordinary environments can consume capacity quickly because they require active filtering, manual social interpretation, task switching, and self-regulation. When capacity is spent, small demands become large because there is no room left to process them.

### Capacity is a processing budget

Capacity is often described as energy, but the more precise frame is processing budget.

Every day requires the brain and body to process demand. Some demand is obvious: work, conflict, deadlines, appointments. Some is invisible: fluorescent lighting, background noise, clothing texture, ambiguous tone, forced eye contact, unplanned transitions, hunger signals that arrive late, the effort of suppressing stims.

For many autistic adults, the invisible category is the expensive one.

The nervous system is not only reacting to dramatic events. It is continuously adapting to environments that were not designed around its thresholds. This means the baseline cost of an ordinary day can be much higher than it appears from the outside.

If someone else spends ten units of capacity in a meeting, an autistic person may spend eighty: the meeting content, the sensory environment, the social decoding, the masking, the transition into the meeting, and the transition back out.

The visible event is the same. The cost is not.

### Allostatic load

The formal stress-physiology term for this accumulation is allostatic load.

Allostasis is how the body maintains stability through change. Heart rate adjusts. Cortisol rises. attention sharpens. The nervous system shifts into a state that can handle demand. This is useful in short bursts.

Allostatic load is what accumulates when those adaptive systems stay engaged too often or for too long. The body keeps paying the cost of adaptation without enough recovery to reset.

For autistic adults, allostatic load can accumulate from ordinary life. Sensory filtering requires effort. Social participation requires calculation. Masking requires self-inhibition. Task switching requires recalibration. None of these has to look dramatic to be physiologically expensive.

This is why burnout can appear mysterious. The person may not be able to point to one disaster. The disaster is the arithmetic.

### Fast fatigue and slow accumulation

Capacity operates on at least two timescales.

Fast fatigue happens within a day. This is the moment when noise becomes less tolerable, choices become harder, words become slower, and minor problems start feeling impossible. Working memory degrades. The prefrontal cortex has less room to coordinate action. The system becomes more reactive because there is less spare capacity available to regulate.

Slow accumulation happens across weeks and months. This is the cost of repeated incomplete recovery. If each day ends with capacity still depleted, the next day begins lower. If that pattern continues, the baseline shrinks. Skills that were previously available become unreliable. Sensory tolerance drops. Executive function degrades. Masking becomes harder to sustain.

Fast fatigue explains the difficult afternoon. Slow accumulation explains burnout.

Both matter because the short-term pattern becomes the long-term pattern when recovery never catches up.

### Why decision fatigue belongs here

Decision fatigue is a capacity signal.

Choices require working memory, prediction, uncertainty tolerance, and inhibition of competing options. When capacity is available, the system can compare, decide, and move. When capacity is low, the same choice becomes a wall.

This is why small questions can feel impossible late in the day. The difficulty is not the question itself. The difficulty is that choosing now requires a control system that has already been used for hours.

Decision fatigue connects directly to allostatic load. Chronic stress and daily micro-decisions both draw down the same regulatory resources. Masking adds more: each social moment can require tiny decisions about expression, tone, movement, timing, eye contact, and whether a natural response is allowed.

An energy accounting system works because it makes these invisible withdrawals visible. It turns "I should be able to do this" into a more accurate question: "What has this already cost, and what capacity remains?"

### Why environmental control belongs here

Environmental control is often treated as a sensory accommodation. It is also a capacity intervention.

Reducing noise, light, clutter, interruption, and ambiguity lowers extraneous cognitive load. The system no longer has to spend working memory filtering the room before it can do the task. More bandwidth remains available for thinking, choosing, communicating, and regulating.

This is why a person may appear to have better executive function in a quiet room than in an open office. The executive system did not become more disciplined. The environment stopped taxing the resources it needed.

Capacity is context-sensitive. Change the load and the person changes.

### Why visual schedules belong here

Visual schedules reduce capacity use by externalizing time and sequence.

Time is abstract. Sequence is abstract. Future steps must be held in working memory unless something outside the brain holds them instead. A visible calendar, checklist, or first-then plan changes the demand: the person no longer has to maintain the whole structure internally.

This is not only useful for children. It is a basic load-reduction strategy for any system whose working memory is easily consumed by sensory, social, or emotional demand.

When a schedule is visible, fewer choices need to be made from scratch. Fewer transitions arrive as surprises. Fewer plans must be reconstructed after interruption. Capacity is preserved.

### Threshold and overflow

Threshold is the point where demand exceeds available capacity.

The threshold moves. It is lower after poor sleep, illness, pain, conflict, sensory exposure, social demand, or previous overflow. It is higher after actual recovery, predictability, low sensory load, and unmasked rest.

This is why the same person can handle an event one week and not handle it the next. The event did not change. The available capacity did.

Overflow can look different depending on the nervous system's response.

Meltdown is active overflow: fight-or-flight activation, loss of top-down control, visible discharge. Shutdown is protective reduction: freeze, speech loss, motor inhibition, withdrawal. Both are capacity failures, not choices. Both require recovery, not argument.

The important point: after overflow, capacity does not instantly return to baseline. The threshold often remains lower. A second demand arrives into a system that is already sensitized. This is why repeated overflow can spiral.

### Burnout

Burnout is sustained capacity failure.

It happens when the system is asked to operate above sustainable load for too long. The result is not just tiredness. It is functional loss: executive function degrades, sensory tolerance drops, social capacity shrinks, speech may become harder, daily tasks may become inaccessible.

Burnout is often misread as depression, laziness, regression, or lack of resilience. It can overlap with depression, but it is not the same mechanism. Burnout is a capacity disorder driven by prolonged allostatic overload and insufficient recovery.

The solution is not pushing harder. Pushing harder is part of what caused it. Recovery requires lowering load, increasing actual rest, reducing masking, changing environments, and rebuilding capacity over time.

### What helps

The capacity model points to three categories of intervention.

Reduce withdrawals. Lower sensory load. Reduce unnecessary choices. Protect focus from interruption. Use scripts. Use visual schedules. Make communication explicit. Remove demands that do not need to be paid today.

Increase deposits. Sleep, solitude, sensory rest, special interests, movement, deep pressure, unmasked time, and predictable routines can restore capacity when they actually reduce load rather than adding performance requirements.

Track the math. Energy accounting works because the nervous system does not always report cost in real time. Listing withdrawals and deposits makes patterns visible before overflow. It shifts the question from "Why did I react like that?" to "What filled the cup?"

### What connects here

The capacity model is the hub that explains why every previous mechanism gets worse under load.

Sensory processing fills capacity through constant filtering. Social processing fills capacity through manual interpretation. Executive function fails when bandwidth is gone. Emotional processing becomes harder when body signals arrive late or all at once. Masking consumes capacity continuously in the background.

The model also explains why strategies work. Environmental control reduces incoming load. Visual schedules reduce working memory demand. Communication scripts reduce spontaneous generation cost. Energy accounting prevents invisible accumulation. Recovery practices increase drain rate.

Capacity is not an excuse. It is the operating limit of the system. Respecting it is how function becomes sustainable.
